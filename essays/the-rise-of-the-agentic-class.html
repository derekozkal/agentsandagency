Agents and Agency · No. 2

The Rise of the Agentic Class

AI and the new digital divide

By Derek Özkal · October 15, 2025 · Originally published on Substack

A few weeks ago I attended the accurately, but poorly named The AI Conference1 in San Francisco. This was quite a bit different than most of the conferences that I would normally attend as most sessions were geared for a technical audience. It was helpful to hear from those building and designing these systems as it complemented where I have more knowledge: economists and management scholars focused on researching the effectiveness of AI and its impact on the economy, firms, workers, etc.

Someone said the key theme of the 2024 AI Conference was “AI agents”. If I had to pick a theme for the 2025 conference it would be “Seriously AI agents everywhere doing everything, k, thanx, bye.” There was more than that of course, but there really seemed to be a focus on how to build better or “safer” agents, or how to integrate AI agents better with existing firm infrastructure and processes, and work with unstructured or structured data using approaches like GraphRAG2. I put “safer” in quotes because at this conference “safe” generally meant security, like making sure that people couldn’t jailbreak these tools, access sensitive data, etc. This had nothing to do with whether or not it was safe to have agents at scale carry out so much work activity. And it certainly didn't address the most critical question of all: when we get to design our own teammates, will we build them to challenge us, or just to agree with us?

Hold Up, What is an AI Agent?

Great question.

This is getting used to describe a lot of related but different activities, but for today, let’s keep it simple: An AI agent refers to some sort of tool that has the capability to reason and act. Think of it as an application of generative AI models that are able to “think” and act towards a set of predefined goals.

Do AI Agents Actually Work Though?

Increasingly so. And there’s already a growing body of research showing significant productivity gains for firms/workers using LLMs and AI agents.3

Counter example thoughts: Maybe you are thinking about how Klarna tried to replace workers with AI and countless other examples where AI has failed. It’s true that there are no shortage of examples of failures that are easy to dismiss, but this is the early days of implementation. There is no playbook for how to implement AI tools into existing organizational structures. It would be hard to deny that some of these tools already work quite well even without firms/workers understanding how to really use them. Even with early missteps, you can immediately see why people are worried about this technology replacing workers.

Will Agents Replace Workers?

Maybe someday, but it’s not as simple as: company fires Employee A and replaces them with AI Agent Z that does the same thing for fewer dollar bucks (Hi Bluey fans!).

The good news for human workers is work is typically done as a bundle of tasks that we call jobs. Having AI tools, like agents, being able to replace certain tasks does not necessarily mean jobs will be eliminated or that work will be automated away. There is not a set amount of work in the world. These tools could vastly expand work. One emerging concern is that a “job” could be replaced by a bundle of AI agents.

Historical Example Alert: ATMs Didn’t Replace Tellers… At Least Not Immediately

You may already know this story, but if not, here’s a very abridged version. With the proliferation of ATMs in the 1970s and 80s, there was fear that the invention of ATMs would replace tellers. This did not happen though. As Jim Bessen and others have shown4, the impact was more nuanced. Initially, ATMs changed duties (less cash handling, more customer service) and made it so that banks could open more branches with fewer tellers. So while each location had fewer tellers, there were enough new branches that teller employment grew for a long time. The rise of internet banking though did start to change this story and employment has really dropped. This has negative economic mobility consequences though. Starting as a teller can be an important first step on the career ladder in the financial industry. This is a role that is predominately filled by women, so this is an important example of how technological change can have larger effects for certain demographic groups.5

Your New Teammate Will Never Come to Happy Hour

Just as ATMs reshaped the tasks within a job, AI agents are set to reshape the very composition of our teams. Certain types of work will now be so much cheaper (using a human plus some number of AI agents or tools) that we now want a lot more of that type of work. That marketing campaign, competitor research, service offering, etc. that is too expensive today (in terms of employee costs, timeline, opportunity cost), might make sense with these new tools. A business with 100 employees that does 10 big things now might be able to do 30 big things with the same number of human employees by using some number of AI agents. So maybe our jobs are safe for a while. But also maybe they are all gone in the long run.6

Designing Work Teams Comprised of Humans and AI Agents

As AI Agent capabilities grow, we may not be far away from the day that work teams and departments are composed of four types of workers: human employees, human independent contractors, firm-owned AI agents, and external AI agents (the AI agent equivalent of independent contractors). If that’s confusing, that’s totally fair. I’m not certain this isn’t an oversimplification, but maybe this table helps.

Mostly Full Firm ControlLimited Firm Control
HumansEmployeesIndependent Contractors
AI AgentsFirm-Owned AI AgentsExternal AI Agents

For example, a marketing team might use its own “Firm-Owned” agent trained on internal sales data while hiring an “External” specialized legal-review agent from a third-party vendor for a specific campaign. The line between internal and external AI agents is much blurrier than the line between employees and contractors.7

Regardless of the precise structures, at some point in the near future, we will have AI agents and tools doing significant amounts of work inside organizations. How will we structure jobs, work duties, career progression and more when teams are part human and part agentic? How will we design AI agents for work? How should we? How different will the answers to those questions be? Are there lessons from the emergence of the Internet and social media? How do humans maintain dignity and agency in this new world?8

One Possibility for the Next Few Years

Let’s go a couple levels into what this all might mean even if the premise (AI agent co-workers) is not happening quite yet. Because not only might it happen, it might happen very soon and we should prepare for what that means.

Here are some very fancy equations about why I think this might happen sooner rather than later…

Firm perspective:

Top performers + AI agents customized to them = lower costs, increased profits, and higher productivity9

The New Elite

We are entering a world where extreme expertise is going to be very powerful and lucrative. I am talking about the new super productive working class (top performers working with powerful and customized AI agents).

How should firms structure their (mostly) AI teams? How are they likely to structure them (sycophancy; social media, algorithms of confirmation bias)?

Good (currently all human) co-workers both challenge and support you as necessary. Highly functioning teams often have members who complement each other in specific ways while also sharing certain values/norms/views that create an environment of trust and intellectual rigor. When you get to literally design and select your own teammates (AI agents), will we design them so that they complement, challenge, and support us? Or will we create echo chambers, uniformity, and confirmation bias? Or will organizational leaders design and select the AI coworkers for our top human performers?

Think about the shows we watch, the music we stream, the personalities we follow. It will be customized to make us happy and engaged. But, that does not mean that it will be good for us.10

Not Who, But How

Given the inertia of the status quo for most organizations coupled with concerns of security and a tremendous lack of understanding what these tools can do, I suspect that most early agentic AI use in firms will be on top of existing team structures. The team of ten is now a team of ten and three customized AI agents to support the team. This might be coupled with not posting or hiring as many interns and entry level positions and not replacing everyone who leaves. I do think that some firms (especially in industries where extreme expertise really matters like law, medicine, research) will start to build more agentic teams around experts. If that works, it’s just a matter of time before others follow.

The restructuring of work by AI agents may be inevitable. When we all have digital co-workers, we will have a single, profound choice: will we build challengers that sharpen our thinking, or sycophants that soothe our egos?

This choice is the mechanism that will create the new digital divide. The new elite (the Agentic Class) will not be defined by who uses AI, but by how they use it. They will be the ones who choose the discomfort of an AI that argues back, that finds flaws in their logic, and that pushes them toward genuine expertise. Everyone else risks being stranded on the wrong side of a new, unbridgeable gap in capability.

The blueprints for this new workforce are being drawn up right now, in code and in conference rooms. The only question that matters is, who gets to hold the pen, er, um, keyboard?

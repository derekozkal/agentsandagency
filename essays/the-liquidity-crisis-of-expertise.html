The Liquidity Crisis of Expertise

Why AI needs cognitive redundancy

By Derek Özkal · January 3, 2026 · Originally published on Substack

I always had lots of questions as a kid. I often didn’t have access to answers for most of them. I grew up scavenging for answers in worn copies of the Guinness Book of World Records. Today, frontier AI offers perfectly customized answers instantly. We have moved from stockpiling information to renting access to it. We have achieved Just-in-Time Cognition.

The Internet brought us access to nearly immediate answers (not always right of course) and has reduced the need to dwell and think hard about solutions. In one sense, frontier AI models are just an extension of this. The magical part is that you get to have a really specific question, inquiry, or thought and then a response that is perfectly customized to your inquiry. This change in the way we think and when we “need” information has some obvious benefits, but also concerns.

Just in Time Manufacturing

The COVID pandemic offered all kinds of lessons, but I want to highlight one: manufacturing shortages. To simplify (but hopefully not oversimplify), global supply chains had been developed with a focus on efficiency1. This means that you try to minimize inventory (buffers, extras, etc.) out of the system to maximize speed. The major tradeoff with this system is that it is fragile. When a shock hits, it shuts down the system as the shock ripples through an entire supply chain that was built for efficiency, not resiliency. This low inventory approach is referred to as “Just-in-Time” manufacturing. Why pay to warehouse a bunch of parts that you don’t need right now? When it works, it saves on costs and increases production speed. The tradeoff of course is that if there is shock somewhere in the chain, everything comes to a halt. This happened with a variety of products during the pandemic from Grape Nuts to toilet paper to yeast.

Just-in-Time Cognition

We are now applying the same concept of just-in-time manufacturing to human intelligence. Human cognition—the process of thinking, knowing, and remembering—is changing2. We are moving from “internalizing” knowledge (stockpiling understanding in our brains) to “retrieving” it on-demand from AI tools. We are maximizing the efficiency of the output while driving the resiliency of the process to zero. We are making the exact same mistake with our minds that we made with the global economy in 2019. By 2030, if we are without AI tools, will we be like a bunch of Waymos during a power outage?3

The Concrete Coastline: Trading Variance for Fragility

Let’s work through another metaphor. Natural systems (like sand dunes) are messy and shifting (high variance), but they absorb the energy of tides and storms. AI tools are like paving over the dunes. We are building a “Concrete Coastline” of automated workflows. It looks cleaner and more efficient. This issue is that concrete doesn’t absorb energy; it resists it until it cracks.

In Antifragile4, Nassim Taleb warns that if you suppress the natural ups and downs of a system, you actually make it fragile. You create a “silent risk” that builds up until it explodes. We are currently doing this with AI by removing the “friction” of human effort.

Humans provide necessary “slack” in a system. We pause, we double-check, and we hesitate when something looks off. AI has no hesitation. It closes the gap between intent and execution entirely. Without that human buffer, a small error in a prompt or a subtle AI hallucination doesn’t get caught. Instead, it propagates instantly. As we move toward autonomous AI agents, we are effectively removing the brakes from a speeding car.

The Liquidity Crisis of Expertise

AI agents make AI tools even more automated. There is a paradox in a world of automated plenty: Our systems appear obscenely rich in output, creating endless code, reports, summaries, and more with just a prompt. We are Asset Rich, gorging on automated output. Yet, we are becoming Solvent Poor in the only currency that stabilizes a system: original human judgment. We are facing a liquidity crisis of expertise. At some point there will be an inevitable “Black Swan” event—a market shock so novel the training data couldn’t even conceive of it—that it shatters an AI Agent’s illusion of competence. Everything locks up. The organization panics and tries to call upon its “Human Capital” to manually engineer a solution, but the human no longer has the skill to do so. Lisanne Bainbridge’s Ironies of Automation5 (from 1983!) is the oldest reference to this point that I could find. Essentially, the smoother the automated system runs, the more indispensable the human is for the truly, novel edge-case failures, and yet the more hopelessly deskilled that human has become from prolonged cognitive idleness. When the inevitable moment arrives to “pay” for a fix with genuine human insight, we find our reserves empty. We bankrupted our critical skills in the rush to buy fleeting, automated speed. We are left with a liquidity crisis of expertise. I like to picture us all as Homer Simpson suddenly seeing alarms go off at the nuclear power plant.

A solution - The Three Pillars of “Cognitive Redundancy”

We are in need of a pivot. I don’t think it is realistic to have a moratorium on AI innovation or expect the rate of advancement at the frontier labs to slow down.

Intentional Friction

Instead, I would like to see a renewed focus on resiliency rather than efficiency. AI and automation can remove friction, which is sometimes great, but it’s also where we have chances to correct course. Let’s stop viewing “friction” as waste. Friction is the premium we pay for survival. We need something akin to cognitive redundancy: the intentional maintenance of ‘inefficient’ human capability to ensure continuity during algorithmic failure. Just as data centers have redundant power generators that sit idle 99% of the time, organizations need redundant cognitive pathways (humans who can do the work manually). Human experience can also be valuable when handling the unknown.6

Analog Drills

What does it really mean to build out cognitive redundancy though? We must be willing to pay more (in time/money) for human work not because it is better, but because it is the insurance policy against systemic collapse. Organizations should mandate “analog weeks7” or introduce “Chaos Monkey” style drills where the AI is turned off, forcing the organization to prove it is still cognitively solvent. Right now, AI isn’t integrated enough into most organizations for this to seem viable, but that day is coming fast.

The Human Baseline

I’m also a big fan of having humans build out frameworks, bullet points, first drafts, etc. before using AI tools. You must have some sort of human baseline before you start evaluating AI generated content. Otherwise, it’s a bit like someone asking for your thoughts on something when it’s already 90% done.

As an adult, I still have a lot of questions. Getting answers today is incredibly fast. I can get answers just-in-time. That’s fine for ad hoc requests or where I don’t care deeply about the topic.8 Whenever the topic is something that matters though, I like to spend some time thinking through the stockpile of information, experiences, and ideas in my own head for a bit first. It might not always be the fastest way to a solution, but it seems like a modest price to pay for building my own intellectual resiliency.

The Apprenticeship Paradox

This “human baseline” is critical for personal resiliency, but it is existential for organizational longevity. We are currently cheering the ability of AI to automate the “drudgery” of junior-level work like summarizing meetings, drafting basic code, and writing memos and briefs.

But for a novice, that drudgery is not waste; it is training.

Struggle is the mechanism of learning. If we allow junior staff to bypass the “messy middle” of problem-solving by jumping straight to a polished AI result, they are effectively skipping the gym. They get the output without the muscle growth.

If the current generation of experts stops doing the manual work because they’ve “earned” the right to use AI, and the next generation never starts doing the manual work because AI does it for them, where does the next generation of experts come from?

We aren’t just creating a liquidity crisis for today; by hollowing out the apprenticeship pipeline, we are ensuring intellectual bankruptcy for tomorrow. Organizations must mandate manual work for juniors not to punish them, but to ensure they have enough “deposits” of experience to draw from when they eventually run the place.

We are well on our way to a world where we are ‘Asset Rich’ in output but bankrupt in judgment. Human agency requires human thought. If we don’t pay the premium for cognitive redundancy now, we won’t be able to afford the bail-out when the system crashes.
